# Eight Patterns for Visual Note-Taking

Visual note-taking is a broad practice; this article enumerates eight
specific patterns that show up in the wild. Each is a distinct technique
with its own affordances and trade-offs. The point of this article is
breadth — to give you a map of the territory before you commit to any
one approach in depth.

## The patterns

1. **Cornell Method** — Split each page into cue / notes / summary
   columns; the cue column drives recall, the summary forces synthesis.
2. **Sketchnoting** — Combine handwritten text with simple drawings,
   icons, and connectors to encode meaning visually and structurally.
3. **Mind Mapping** — Radial tree of ideas anchored on a central node,
   useful for brainstorming and decomposition.
4. **Outline Method** — Strict indentation hierarchy with bullets;
   linear, easy to type, hard to scan.
5. **Boxing Method** — Group related notes into bordered boxes per
   topic, optimised for visual scanning and chunking.
6. **Charting Method** — Tabular notes with predefined columns per
   attribute; ideal for comparison-heavy material.
7. **Mapping Method** — Connect concepts with explicit labelled edges
   (similar to mind mapping but with named relationships).
8. **Zettelkasten** — Atomic notes interlinked by reference, with no
   strict hierarchy; the network IS the structure.

## When to use which

For lectures with a clear topical structure, Cornell or Outline work
best — the speaker imposes structure and you mirror it. For brainstorming
sessions and creative work, Mind Mapping and Sketchnoting are the
strongest options because they don't force linear thinking. For
research projects where you'll return to notes weeks later, Zettelkasten
wins on long-term recall because every note is queryable independently.

Charting is underrated — for any topic with multiple competing options
along the same axes (frameworks, tools, methodologies), a chart is the
fastest way to capture and compare. Boxing and Mapping are middle
ground; they work well for personal study notes that the author will
revisit but no-one else will read.

## Common thread

All eight patterns share one insight: the layout encodes meaning. A
linear bulleted list says "ordered". A radial map says "centred". A
labelled graph says "relational". Choosing the pattern is choosing the
structure of your thinking. None is universally best.
